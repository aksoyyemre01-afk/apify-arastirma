## Post 1 — Personal story + lesson
**Format:** highest comment rate · **Length:** ~1,150 characters

We shut down our best-performing channel.

For months the report told the same story: this channel drove the most leads.
Most of the budget went there. Nobody questioned it, because the dashboard was green.

Then at the end of a quarter someone from sales asked:
"How many of those leads actually turn into meetings?"

Nobody knew.

We checked. The channel produced [X] leads, but only [Y%] became qualified
meetings. The other channel brought in a third of the volume — and generated
[Z%] of closed revenue.

We'd been chasing the wrong number for two quarters.

We moved the budget. Lead volume dropped, and the chart looked worse.
Pipeline grew [W%].

Three things I took from it:

A green metric isn't the same as the right metric.
Marketing can't grade its own homework — sales' numbers do that.
And the most dangerous metric is the one nobody questions.

Now we open every quarter with one question:
"If this number goes up, does the company actually win?"

When did you last audit the metric you're optimising for?

## Post 2 — Framework / list
**Format:** highest save rate · **Length:** ~1,100 characters

The fight between sales and marketing usually starts with the same sentence:

"The leads you're sending us are garbage."

We heard it too. So we stopped arguing in meetings and wrote a checklist instead.
Before a lead reaches sales, five things have to be clear:

1. **Problem** — Did they describe the problem in their own words?
2. **Authority** — Are they at the decision table, or reporting to it?
3. **Timing** — "This quarter" or "sometime, maybe"?
4. **Alternative** — How are they solving it today? (If the answer is "we're not," it's a hard sell.)
5. **Touch** — What have they seen from us before? First touch or fifth?

If fewer than four are clear, the lead doesn't go to sales. It goes to nurture.

Lead volume dropped [X%] in month one. Sales was not happy — then it landed.
Meeting conversion went from [Y%] to [Z%]. Same team, same budget.

The difference: marketing stopped reporting "how many filled the form"
and started reporting "how many are ready to talk."

What's the one question you ask before handing a lead to sales?

## Post 3 — Contrarian take
**Format:** highest discussion · **Length:** ~950 characters

Unpopular opinion: you don't have a content volume problem. You have a targeting problem.

Every week there's a meeting about publishing more.
The calendar fills up, the agency delivers, the output count climbs.
Then the quarter ends and pipeline hasn't moved.

Last quarter we tested the opposite. We cut publishing volume in half
and pointed the leftover capacity at one thing: content that answers the
objections our sales team actually hears on calls.

We didn't brainstorm a single new idea. We listened to recorded calls and
wrote up the [X] objections that came up most.

Traffic went down.
Qualified conversations went up [Y%].

Because nobody reads a "2026 trends" post and buys something.
People buy when they find an answer at the exact point they hesitated.

Content marketing isn't a publishing job. It's an objection-handling job.

Tell me I'm wrong — did volume or focus move the needle for you?
